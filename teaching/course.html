Numerical methods for PDEs in Python: a 14-week lab course

One two-hour lab a week. Each week has a topic, a task for the session and the sections of the guide that contain the worked code.

What students can do afterwards

  • Turn a physical question into a model with units, boundary conditions and a stated quantity of interest.
  • Choose between finite differences, finite volumes and finite elements for a given problem and justify the choice with measured error and cost.
  • Verify a result against an exact solution, a balance or a refinement study, and say what the check does not show.
  • Choose and tune linear, nonlinear and time-integration methods and read their reports.

Prerequisites

Basic Python with NumPy and Matplotlib, and a first course in differential equations. No earlier contact with finite elements is assumed.

Week by week

WeekTopic, task and material
1The modelling sequence
Set up the environment, then state a question, assemble a model, solve it and check it: the radiative cooling of one thermal mass against its closed-form solution.
  • Make the notebook tell a scientific story
  • Radiative cooling of one thermal mass
2Time integration
Reuse the transient workflow for a damped oscillator, then separate the temporal error from the physical model by halving the step.
  • Reuse the transient workflow for a damped oscillator
  • Separate temporal error from the physical model
3Finite differences
Measure the order of finite-difference stencils on a travelling wave and compare it with the formal order.
  • Finite differences
  • Measure finite-difference order on a traveling wave
4Finite volumes
Conservative reconstruction and slope limiting; transport of a periodic wave with three flux operators.
  • Finite volumes and finite integration
  • Conservative reconstruction and slope limiting
  • Transport a periodic wave with three flux operators
5Finite elements
Compare element orders on a manufactured harmonic field; L2 projection against nodal interpolation.
  • Finite elements
  • Compare finite-element orders on a manufactured harmonic field
  • L2 projection versus nodal interpolation
6One problem, every method
Solve one 3D Poisson problem with FEM, FVM and FDM on the same mesh and grade each against the exact solution.
  • Change one numerical choice at a time
  • One model, five discretizations (example)
7Linear solvers
Direct and iterative solvers and preconditioners against a common direct reference.
  • Linear solvers and preconditioners
  • Linear solvers against a common direct reference
8Nonlinear solvers
Solve the same nonlinear implicit cooling stage with different nonlinear solvers and compare iterations and residuals.
  • Nonlinear solvers
  • Solve the same nonlinear implicit cooling stage
9Elasticity in 3D
A clamped beam under load: deflection and stress, a two-mesh comparison and beam theory as an approximate reference.
  • Three-dimensional cantilever
  • A 3D cantilever (example)
10Heat and magnetic fields
Heat transport with prescribed recirculation and its heat balance; the magnetic field of a homogenized solenoid.
  • Heat transport with prescribed recirculation
  • Magnetostatics of a homogenized solenoid
11Coupled problems
Monolithic against partitioned thermal exchange; copying, interpolating and conservatively remapping a field.
  • Compare monolithic and partitioned thermal exchange
  • Copy, interpolate and conservatively remap on a line
12Your own equation
Derive a forcing from a chosen exact solution, solve a reaction–diffusion equation on three meshes and read the measured order.
  • Derive a verification target and its forcing
  • Read the measured refinement results
  • Your own equation (example)
13–14Project
Each student picks a first case from one physics module and turns it into a verified notebook with a stated question, a check and a figure.
  • Keep verification and presentation together
  • Repository examples
  • The sixteen physics modules (example)

A way to assess it

  • Eight exercise sheets, each handed in as a notebook that runs from top to bottom.
  • One project in weeks 13 and 14: a verified notebook on a physics module of the student's choice, presented in ten minutes.
  • Grade the check as much as the result: a wrong number with a check that exposes it is worth more than a right number without one.

Shorter or different

For a seven-week block, take weeks 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9 and 12. For a course on computational physics instead of numerics, keep weeks 1, 2, 6 and 9 to 14 and let the students spend more time on the physics modules.
