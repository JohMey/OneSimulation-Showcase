Potential profile in an electrochemical cell

The potential across a zinc-copper cell at open circuit, during discharge and during charge, with the steps at the electrodes and the ohmic drop in between.

Current distributionButler-VolmerClosed-form check
Download the notebook (.ipynb)Download the script (.py)

What it computes

OneSimulation solves the electrolyte current balance with ElectrolyteCurrentDistribution between two planar electrodes, once with Butler-Volmer kinetics (secondary distribution) and once without (primary), and sweeps the cell voltage for the polarisation curve.

What it checks

The currents match the closed-form voltage budget (ohmic drop plus two arsinh overpotentials) within 2e-11 A/m^2 up to 680 A/m^2; at 0.80 V the 1.10 V open-circuit voltage splits into 158 mV ohmic drop, 53 mV at zinc and 89 mV at copper.

The notebook

The complete notebook, cell by cell. Run it to produce the figures and the comparison printout.

A battery or galvanic cell delivers its open-circuit voltage only when no current flows. Under load the cell voltage is lower, during charging it is higher, and the difference is spent in three places: the activation overpotentials at the two electrodes and the ohmic drop in the electrolyte between them. This example makes that budget visible as a potential profile across a Daniell-type cell,

\[\mathrm{Zn}\,|\,\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\,\|\,\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\,|\,\mathrm{Cu},\qquad E^0_\mathrm{Zn}=-0.76\ \mathrm{V},\quad E^0_\mathrm{Cu}=+0.34\ \mathrm{V},\quad U_\mathrm{OCV}=1.10\ \mathrm{V}.\]

In the electrolyte (uniform composition, conductivity \(\kappa\)) the current density is \(\mathbf i=-\kappa\nabla\phi_l\) with \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf i=0\). At each electrode the metal potential \(\phi_s\), the electrolyte potential \(\phi_l\) and the equilibrium potential \(E_\mathrm{eq}\) define the overpotential \(\eta=\phi_s-\phi_l-E_\mathrm{eq}\), and the current follows Butler-Volmer kinetics with \(\alpha_a=\alpha_c=1/2\):

\[i=2\,i_0\sinh\!\Big(\frac{nF\eta}{2RT}\Big)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \eta=\frac{2RT}{nF}\,\operatorname{arsinh}\frac{i}{2i_0}.\]

Without kinetics (\(i_0\to\infty\), primary current distribution) \(\eta=0\). OneSimulation solves the current balance with ElectrolyteCurrentDistribution on a line between the two electrodes.

| Boundary | Condition | |---|---| | \(x=0\), zinc | \(\phi_s=0\) (ground), \(E_\mathrm{eq}=-0.76\) V, \(i_0=20\) A/m² | | \(x=L\), copper | \(\phi_s=U\) (cell voltage), \(E_\mathrm{eq}=+0.34\) V, \(i_0=5\) A/m² |

Reference. J. Newman and N. P. Balsara, Electrochemical Systems, 4th ed., Wiley 2021, ch. 8 and 18; A. J. Bard and L. R. Faulkner, Electrochemical Methods, 2nd ed., Wiley 2001, ch. 3. For a planar cell the current density \(i\) (positive on discharge) satisfies the voltage budget

\[U_\mathrm{OCV}-U=\frac{iL}{\kappa}+\frac{2RT}{nF}\Big[\operatorname{arsinh}\frac{i}{2i_{0,\mathrm{Zn}}} +\operatorname{arsinh}\frac{i}{2i_{0,\mathrm{Cu}}}\Big],\]

which reduces to Ohm's law \(i=\kappa\,(U_\mathrm{OCV}-U)/L\) for the primary distribution. The electrolyte potential is linear between the electrodes, and at open circuit it sits \(0.76\) V above the zinc metal.

import numpy as np
from onesim.cad.d1 import Line
from onesim.meshing import cartesian
from onesim.physics.chemistry import CurrentElectrode, ElectrolyteCurrentDistribution
from onesim.visualization import plots, theme
from scipy.optimize import brentq

theme.set()

Cell

A 5 mm electrolyte gap with a conductivity of 5 S/m (about that of a molar zinc or copper sulphate solution), two-electron reactions at 25 °C. The exchange current densities are illustrative values for the two metals.

gap, conductivity, temperature, electrons = 5e-3, 5.0, 298.15, 2
zinc, copper, open_circuit = -0.76, 0.34, 1.10
exchange = {"zinc": 20.0, "copper": 5.0}
thermal = 8.314462618 * temperature / 96485.33212
line = Line(start=(0.0, 0.0, 0.0), end=(gap, 0.0, 0.0))
mesh = cartesian(shape=(40,), extents=(gap,), geometry=line, periodic=False).build()
print(
    f"electrolyte resistance L/κ = {gap / conductivity * 1e4:.1f} Ω cm², RT/F = {1e3 * thermal:.2f} mV"
)

Three operating points and a polarisation curve

Open circuit (1.10 V), discharge at 0.80 V and charge at 1.40 V, each with kinetics (secondary distribution) and without (primary distribution); then a sweep of the cell voltage from 0.2 V to 1.6 V.

cells = {
    (voltage, kinetics): ElectrolyteCurrentDistribution(
        conductivity=conductivity,
        temperature=temperature,
        electrodes={
            "start": CurrentElectrode(
                potential=0.0,
                equilibrium_potential=zinc,
                electron_number=electrons,
                exchange_current_density=exchange["zinc"] if kinetics else None,
            ),
            "end": CurrentElectrode(
                potential=voltage,
                equilibrium_potential=copper,
                electron_number=electrons,
                exchange_current_density=exchange["copper"] if kinetics else None,
            ),
        },
    ).solve(mesh)
    for voltage in (*np.round(np.linspace(0.2, 1.6, 15), 2), 1.4)
    for kinetics in (True, False)
}
centres = (np.arange(40) + 0.5) * gap / 40

Comparison with the voltage budget

The current from the closed-form budget, the overpotentials and the ohmic drop at the three operating points; the electrolyte potentials at the electrode surfaces come from the solution.

budget = lambda u: brentq(
    lambda i: i * gap / conductivity
    + 2
    * thermal
    / electrons
    * (
        np.arcsinh(i / (2 * exchange["zinc"]))
        + np.arcsinh(i / (2 * exchange["copper"]))
    )
    - (open_circuit - u),
    -1e4,
    1e4,
)
print(
    " U [V]   kinetics   current [A/m²]   budget [A/m²]   η_Zn [mV]   η_Cu [mV]   ohmic drop [mV]   φ_l at Zn [V]"
)
for voltage in (1.1, 0.8, 1.4):
    for kinetics in (True, False):
        result = cells[(voltage, kinetics)]
        current = result.electrode_currents["end"]
        surface = result.electrode_potentials
        print(
            f"{voltage:5.2f} {'BV' if kinetics else 'none':>9s} {current:15.4f} {budget(voltage) if kinetics else conductivity * (open_circuit - voltage) / gap:15.4f} "
            f"{1e3 * (0.0 - surface['start'] - zinc):11.2f} {1e3 * (voltage - surface['end'] - copper):11.2f} "
            f"{1e3 * (surface['start'] - surface['end']):17.2f} {float(surface['start']):15.4f}"
        )
sweep = np.round(np.linspace(0.2, 1.6, 15), 2)
computed = np.array([cells[(u, True)].electrode_currents["end"] for u in sweep])
print(
    f"polarisation curve: largest deviation from the budget {np.abs(computed - [budget(u) for u in sweep]).max():.1e} A/m² "
    f"(currents up to {np.abs(computed).max():.0f} A/m²); largest linearity defect of φ_l "
    f"{max(np.abs(np.diff(r.potential, 2)).max() for r in cells.values()):.1e} V"
)

Figure

The potential across the cell (metal, electrolyte, metal): the steps at the electrodes are the equilibrium potentials plus the overpotentials, the slope in the electrolyte is the ohmic drop. On the right the cell voltage against the current, with and without electrode kinetics. Dotted verticals: the electrode surfaces.

states = {"open circuit, 1.10 V": 1.1, "discharge, 0.80 V": 0.8, "charge, 1.40 V": 1.4}
fig = plots.figure(1, 2, size=(15, 5.5))
plots.line(
    y={
        name: (
            1e3 * np.r_[-1.5e-3, 0.0, 0.0, centres, gap, gap, gap + 1.5e-3],
            np.r_[
                0.0,
                0.0,
                cells[(u, True)].electrode_potentials["start"],
                cells[(u, True)].potential,
                cells[(u, True)].electrode_potentials["end"],
                u,
                u,
            ],
        )
        for name, u in states.items()
    },
    vlines=[0.0, 1e3 * gap],
    xlabel="position x [mm] (zinc metal | electrolyte | copper metal)",
    ylabel="potential [V] (zinc metal = 0)",
    title="Potential profile with Butler-Volmer kinetics",
    colors={"open circuit, 1.10 V": 6, "discharge, 0.80 V": 1, "charge, 1.40 V": 0},
    legend="lower right",
    ax=fig[0],
)
plots.line(
    points={
        "secondary (Butler-Volmer)": (computed, sweep),
        "primary (no kinetics)": (
            np.array([cells[(u, False)].electrode_currents["end"] for u in sweep]),
            sweep,
        ),
    },
    reference={
        "secondary (Butler-Volmer)": (
            np.array([budget(u) for u in np.linspace(0.2, 1.6, 141)]),
            np.linspace(0.2, 1.6, 141),
        ),
        "primary (no kinetics)": (
            conductivity * (open_circuit - np.linspace(0.2, 1.6, 141)) / gap,
            np.linspace(0.2, 1.6, 141),
        ),
    },
    hlines={"open-circuit voltage": open_circuit},
    vlines=[0.0],
    xlabel="current density i [A/m²] (discharge > 0)",
    ylabel="cell voltage U [V]",
    title="Polarisation curve (dashed: voltage budget)",
    legend="upper right",
    ax=fig[1],
)
plots.show()

Going further

Raise the exchange current densities and the secondary curve approaches the primary straight line; lower them and the activation losses dominate at small currents (the Tafel regime, \(\eta\approx\frac{RT}{\alpha nF}\ln(i/i_0)\)). In two or three dimensions the same model gives the primary and secondary current distributions over shaped electrodes, where the Wagner number \(\kappa\,(d\eta/di)/L\) decides how uniform the current is. Concentration overpotentials need the tertiary model with species transport (PoissonNernstPlanck).
