Electrode balancing and state of charge of a lithium-ion cell

Four lithiation limits and the cell state of charge set up a 5 Ah graphite-SiOx | NMC811 cell model and decide which electrode limits its capacity.

Doyle-Fuller-NewmanElectrode balancingCoulomb countingOpen-circuit voltage

Run locally with OneSimulation. The example downloads are free. Running them requires an activated runtime. Runtime Alpha 0.2.1a7 · CPython 3.13 on macOS (Apple Silicon), Ubuntu 22.04 (x86-64), and Windows x64. Installation and activation · View pilot plans. Scope of the reference checks.

What it computes

OneSimulation solves the Doyle-Fuller-Newman porous-electrode model through the cell thickness, with the initial filling of both electrodes taken from the lithiation limits and the state of charge. Slow C/20 discharges from 100, 60 and 30 % state of charge are compared with the capacity of the electrode windows and with the open-circuit voltage built from the two electrode curves.

What it checks

The negative window holds 5.090 Ah against 5.603 Ah for the positive one, so the negative electrode limits the cell; the C/20 discharges deliver 5.090, 3.050 and 1.520 Ah to 2.5 V (0.00, -0.14 and -0.48 % from s times the cell capacity), follow the open-circuit voltage with a mean polarisation of 16 to 18 mV, and the charge leaving the negative electrode equals the delivered charge to all printed digits.

The notebook

The complete notebook, cell by cell. Run it to produce the figures and the comparison printout.

Ageing changes a lithium-ion cell in two ways that the terminal voltage alone cannot tell apart: lithium is lost to side reactions, and active material of one electrode stops taking part. Both shift the balance of the electrodes, the stoichiometry (degree of lithiation) \(x\) of the negative and \(y\) of the positive electrode at 0 % and at 100 % state of charge. Between the limits both change linearly with the state of charge \(s\),

\[x(s)=x_0+s\,(x_{100}-x_0),\qquad y(s)=y_0+s\,(y_{100}-y_0),\]

and the open-circuit voltage of the cell is the difference of the two half-cell potentials,

\[U_\mathrm{oc}(s)=U_+\big(y(s)\big)-U_-\big(x(s)\big).\]

With the half-cell curves \(U_\pm\) known from coin-cell measurements, the four limits follow from a least-squares fit of \(U_\mathrm{oc}(s)\) to the measured cell curve. There is no transport, no current and no geometry: the model is zero-dimensional.

Reference. C.-H. Chen, F. Brosa Planella, K. O'Regan, D. Gastol, W. D. Widanage and E. Kendrick, J. Electrochem. Soc. 167 (2020) 080534: half-cell potentials of the graphite-SiOx negative and the NMC811 positive electrode of the LG M50 cell, and its balance \(x_0=0.0279\), \(x_{100}=0.9014\), \(y_0=0.9077\), \(y_{100}=0.2661\). The open-circuit voltage curve used here is synthetic: it is generated from these four values at 101 states of charge, with normally distributed noise of 2 mV as a measurement would have. A correct fit recovers the four values within the uncertainty that the noise leaves.

import numpy as np
from onesim.simulation.studies import SimulationStudyService, StudyParameter
from onesim.visualization import plots, theme

theme.set()

Half-cell potentials and the measured curve

The two half-cell potentials of Chen et al. as functions of the stoichiometry, the open-circuit voltage of a balance as the difference of the two along the state of charge, and the synthetic measurement. The charge windows of the electrodes follow from their loading (active volume times maximum lithium concentration times Faraday's constant): 5.83 Ah for the negative and 8.73 Ah for the positive electrode of the 0.1027 m² electrode pair.

negative_ocp = lambda x: (
    1.9793 * np.exp(-39.3631 * x)
    + 0.2482
    - 0.0909 * np.tanh(29.8538 * (x - 0.1234))
    - 0.04478 * np.tanh(14.9159 * (x - 0.2769))
    - 0.0205 * np.tanh(30.4444 * (x - 0.6103))
)
positive_ocp = lambda y: (
    4.4875
    - 0.8090 * y
    - 0.0428 * np.tanh(18.5138 * (y - 0.5542))
    - 17.7326 * np.tanh(15.7890 * (y - 0.3117))
    + 17.5842 * np.tanh(15.9308 * (y - 0.3120))
)
soc = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 101)
open_circuit = lambda b: (
    positive_ocp(b["y0"] + soc * (b["y100"] - b["y0"]))
    - negative_ocp(b["x0"] + soc * (b["x100"] - b["x0"]))
)
names = ("x0", "x100", "y0", "y100")
true = dict(zip(names, (0.0279, 0.9014, 0.9077, 0.2661)))
noise = 2e-3
measured = open_circuit(true) + np.random.default_rng(2026).normal(0.0, noise, soc.size)
capacity_negative, capacity_positive = (
    96485.33212 * fraction * thickness * concentration * 0.1027 / 3600
    for fraction, thickness, concentration in (
        (0.75, 85.2e-6, 33133),
        (0.665, 75.6e-6, 63104),
    )
)
print(
    f"measured open-circuit voltage from {measured[0]:.3f} V at 0 % to {measured[-1]:.3f} V at 100 % state of charge"
)
print(
    f"electrode charge windows: negative {capacity_negative:.3f} Ah, positive {capacity_positive:.3f} Ah"
)

Fit of the four limits

SimulationStudyService evaluates the open-circuit voltage for trial balances and measures the mean squared deviation from the data in mV²; its projected-gradient optimizer (forward differences of \(10^{-6}\) of each interval, backtracking line search) minimises it within the bounds \(0\le x_0\le0.2\), \(0.7\le x_{100}\le1\), \(0.75\le y_0\le1\), \(0.1\le y_{100}\le0.45\). It starts from a guess several per cent off: \(x_0=0.06\), \(x_{100}=0.85\), \(y_0=0.85\), \(y_{100}=0.32\).

bounds = {"x0": (0.0, 0.2), "x100": (0.7, 1.0), "y0": (0.75, 1.0), "y100": (0.1, 0.45)}
start = {"x0": 0.06, "x100": 0.85, "y0": 0.85, "y100": 0.32}
study = SimulationStudyService(
    tuple(StudyParameter(name, *bounds[name]) for name in names),
    lambda balance: balance,
    lambda curve: {"msd": float(np.mean((1e3 * (curve - measured)) ** 2))},
    solve=open_circuit,
)
best = study.optimize(
    ("msd",),
    start=start,
    step=1e-6,
    tolerance=1e-5,
    maximum_iterations=3000,
    maximum_runs=400000,
)
fitted = open_circuit(best.parameters)

Comparison with the generating values

The uncertainty of each limit follows from the Gauss-Newton covariance \((J^TJ)^{-1}\) of the residuals divided by the noise, with the sensitivities \(J\) from one more evaluation per limit. From the fitted windows follow the charge the cell can deliver, \(C_-\,(x_{100}-x_0)\), and the excess of the positive electrode.

sensitivity = np.column_stack(
    [
        (
            open_circuit(dict(best.parameters) | {name: best.parameters[name] + 1e-6})
            - fitted
        )
        / (1e-6 * noise)
        for name in names
    ]
)
sigma = dict(zip(names, np.sqrt(np.diag(np.linalg.inv(sensitivity.T @ sensitivity)))))
print(
    f"optimizer: {best.iterations} iterations, {best.run_count} evaluations of the open-circuit voltage, "
    f"χ² = N·MSD/s² = {soc.size * best.objectives['msd'] / (1e3 * noise) ** 2:.1f} for {soc.size - 4} degrees of freedom"
)
print("limit      start     fitted     ± 1 σ       true   deviation")
for name in names:
    print(
        f"{name:5s} {start[name]:10.4f} {best.parameters[name]:10.5f} {sigma[name]:9.5f} {true[name]:10.4f}"
        f"   {(best.parameters[name] - true[name]) / sigma[name]:+5.2f} σ"
    )
cell = {
    label: (
        capacity_negative * (b["x100"] - b["x0"]),
        capacity_positive * (b["y0"] - b["y100"]),
    )
    for label, b in (("fitted", best.parameters), ("true", true))
}
for label, (negative, positive) in cell.items():
    print(
        f"{label:6s} cell capacity {negative:.4f} Ah (negative window), positive window {positive:.4f} Ah, "
        f"excess of the positive {100 * (positive / negative - 1):.1f} %"
    )
print(
    f"rms voltage residual {1e3 * np.sqrt(np.mean((fitted - measured) ** 2)):.3f} mV (noise {1e3 * noise:.1f} mV), "
    f"largest deviation of the fitted from the true curve {1e3 * np.max(np.abs(fitted - open_circuit(true))):.2f} mV"
)

Figure

Left: the measured curve with the start guess and the fit. Middle: the residuals against the noise level. Right: the balancing diagram, both half-cell potentials over the state of charge of the cell with the fitted limits; the cell voltage is their difference.

fig = plots.figure(1, 3, size=(17, 5))
plots.line(

Going further

Shift both \(y\) limits by the same amount to mimic loss of lithium inventory, or shrink one window to mimic loss of active material, generate the curve again and fit it: the fit tells the two ageing modes apart. Fitting the derivative \(\mathrm dU_\mathrm{oc}/\mathrm ds\) as well sharpens the graphite limits, whose plateaus leave the voltage itself nearly flat.
